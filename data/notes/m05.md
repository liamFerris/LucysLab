# Module 5: How much radiation do you use?

*Radiation dose and risk*

## What you should be able to do

- Describe the various dose terms and units.
- Describe the radiation doses used in dentistry and the magnitude of the risks involved.
- Understand the principles of dose limitation.

## Dose terms and units

### Radiation absorbed dose

<mark>This is the total amount of radiation absorbed by the patient. The unit is the gray (Gy).</mark>

### Equivalent dose

<mark>Different types of radiation can cause different levels of damage to the patient's cells. Equivalent dose takes this into account by giving each type of radiation a weighting factor (W<sub>R</sub>) according to the amount of damage it does. The W<sub>R</sub> for X-rays is 1. The unit for equivalent dose is the sievert (Sv).</mark>

<mark>Equivalent dose = radiation absorbed dose × radiation weighting factor</mark>

### Effective dose

Some tissues are more susceptible to radiation damage than others. <mark>Effective dose takes this into account by giving each tissue a weighting factor (W<sub>T</sub>) according to its sensitivity to radiation damage. The unit is also the sievert (Sv).</mark>

<mark>Effective dose = equivalent dose × tissue weighting factor</mark>

Note that <mark>gonad tissue</mark>, red bone marrow and lung tissue are the most sensitive to radiation damage. The nearest sensitive tissue to the area affected by dental radiographs is the thyroid gland.

> **Lucy's note:** Gonad tissue means the reproductive cells.

<mark>Effective dose is the most useful dose in terms of radiology, as it allows us to compare the effects of different types of radiation on different parts of the body.</mark>

### Summary of dose terms

| Dose term | What it takes into account | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| Radiation absorbed dose | Total amount of radiation absorbed by the patient | Gray (Gy) |
| Equivalent dose | Type of radiation (radiation weighting factor, W<sub>R</sub>) | Sievert (Sv) |
| Effective dose | Sensitivity of each tissue (tissue weighting factor, W<sub>T</sub>) | Sievert (Sv) |

### Smaller units

<mark>A sievert is quite a large dose of radiation, so it is more common to hear about:</mark>

| Symbol | Name | Size |
|---|---|---|
| <mark>mSv</mark> | <mark>millisievert</mark> | <mark>One thousandth of a sievert</mark> |
| <mark>µSv</mark> | <mark>microsievert</mark> | <mark>One millionth of a sievert</mark> |

## Effective doses in dental radiography

- <mark>Using modern equipment massively lowers the dose to the patient, when compared with using traditional kit.</mark> Hence the regulations and recommendations on equipment.
- Medical exposures have far higher effective doses than dental exposures:

| Exposure | Effective dose |
|---|---|
| Chest CT scan | 6.6 mSv |
| Barium meal | 1.5 mSv |
| Chest X-ray | 0.038 mSv |
| <mark>Intra-oral dental</mark> | <mark>0.0003 mSv</mark> |

## Natural background radiation dose

<mark>We receive natural doses of radiation all the time: about 2.7 mSv each year in the UK. Compare this figure with those for the medical exposures listed above. A modern panoramic X-ray gives the same dose as two days of natural background radiation.</mark>

## Reference dose

This is the maximum <mark>absorbed dose</mark> allowed for a particular medical or dental examination. <mark>If the absorbed dose given to the patient exceeds this limit, then steps must be taken to reduce further doses.</mark>

## Radiation dose limits

These limits are set to protect various groups of people from excessive radiation doses. For patients who have a clear clinical need for an X-ray investigation, there are no set dose limits.

<mark>Annual dose limits are set for the general public and people who work with radiation, including you.</mark>

| Group | Annual dose |
|---|---|
| Employees (legal limit) | <mark>20 mSv per year</mark> |
| Members of the public, and carers/comforters (legal limit) | <mark>1 mSv per year</mark> |
| All dental staff (recommended dose constraint) | <mark>0.3 mSv per year</mark> |

> **Correction:** The printed handout says "The yearly, legal limit for non-classified workers is 20mSv per year". This has been changed to describe 20 mSv as the legal annual limit for employees because 20 mSv is the limit for employees in general, and under IRR17 a worker likely to receive more than 6 mSv a year must be designated as classified.

<mark>While these are the *legal* upper limits, the *recommended* annual dose constraint for all dental staff is 0.3 mSv.</mark>

Your exposure to X-ray radiation at work can be measured using a personal monitoring device.

## Magnitude of the risks involved

<mark>The younger the patient, the greater the risk.</mark>

### Estimated risk of fatal cancer from an intra-oral radiograph

| Equipment | Risk |
|---|---|
| Traditional equipment | 1 in 1 million exposures |
| <mark>Recommended equipment</mark> | <mark>1 in 10 million exposures</mark> |

### Estimated risk of fatal cancer from an extra-oral radiograph

| Equipment | Risk |
|---|---|

## Digital imaging

There are two main digital imaging systems.

### 1. Solid-state receptors (real-time or "corded" systems)

<mark>These electronic receptors produce an instantaneous image.</mark> <mark>They convert X-ray energy into electrical signals which are then used to construct a digital radiographic image.</mark>

### 2. Phosphor plate receptors (storage plate or "cordless" systems)

<mark>These receptors store the image on a phosphor plate which is then scanned by a laser reader.</mark> <mark>The reader converts the stored image into electrical signals which are then used to construct a digital image.</mark> <mark>Readers should be placed in a dimly lit room.</mark>

### 2D digital images

<mark>2D digital images are made up of pixels.</mark> <mark>Each pixel is assigned a shade of grey depending on the amount of radiation that reached that part of the sensor.</mark> <mark>The size of each pixel influences the resolution (or clarity) of the final image.</mark>

<mark>Digital images can be altered using computer software.</mark> We can change the brightness and contrast, or even add false colouring.

### Advantages and disadvantages of digital imaging

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| <mark>Lower radiation dose to patient</mark> | <mark>Can be expensive</mark> |
| <mark>Elimination of film processing</mark> | <mark>Sensors may be bulky and easily damaged</mark> |
| <mark>Rapid image production</mark> | <mark>Image manipulation possible</mark> |
| <mark>Image manipulation possible</mark> | |
| <mark>Automated image analysis</mark> | |
| <mark>Electronic storage and archiving</mark> | |

Note that image manipulation appears on both sides: it can be useful, but it also means images can be altered.

### Current recommendations for digital imaging

- <mark>Sensor sizes should be available in a range that is comparable to dental film.</mark>
- <mark>Sensitivity of the detector system should be compatible with the X-ray set being used</mark>, ideally DC (constant potential), allowing very short exposure times.
- <mark>Because of the ease with which radiographs can be retaken, it is essential to ensure that all retakes are properly justified, recorded and included in QA statistics.</mark>

### 3D digital images

<mark>Cone Beam Computed Tomography (CBCT) allows us to see a 3D image of the patient.</mark> <mark>The CBCT machine images a cylindrical or spherical part of the patient called the field of view (FOV).</mark> <mark>The image is made up of tiny cubes or voxels</mark>, which are much the same as the pixels in 2D images. <mark>The voxels are reconstructed by computer to show a variety of views.</mark>

## Radiographic film

### Direct-action film (intra-oral)

<mark>Direct-action intra-oral films come in a variety of sizes, colours and speeds.</mark> <mark>D, E and F refer to the speed of the film, F being the fastest type.</mark>

Each film packet contains:

- <mark>A clear plastic barrier envelope.</mark>
- <mark>An outer wrapper (white/colour coded).</mark>
- <mark>Plastic film embossed on one corner for orientation when viewing.</mark>
- <mark>Black paper on either side of the film.</mark>
- <mark>A thin sheet of embossed lead foil.</mark> This helps stop X-rays from passing through the film and into the other side of the patient. It also helps prevent scattered X-ray photons from reducing the quality of the image.
- The plastic film is coated on both sides with a green emulsion. <mark>This emulsion is primarily sensitive to X-rays, but it is also sensitive to light.</mark>

### Current recommendations for intra-oral film

- <mark>The fastest available film consistent with diagnostic results should be used, ideally E or F speed.</mark>
- <mark>"Instant process films" should be limited to specific essential situations</mark> (e.g. during surgery or endodontics).

### Indirect-action film and intensifying screens (extra-oral films)

<mark>Indirect-action film must be used in conjunction with an intensifying screen, because the emulsion in this type of film is sensitive to various colours of light, not X-rays.</mark>

<mark>The intensifying screens consist of fluorescent phosphors embedded in a plastic matrix.</mark> The phosphors include:

| Phosphor | Light emitted |
|---|---|
| <mark>Calcium tungstate</mark> | Blue |
| <mark>Rare earth materials</mark> | Green, ultraviolet or red |

<mark>The film is sandwiched tightly between two intensifying screens inside a light-tight cassette.</mark>

> **Correction:** The printed handout says "light-sensitive cassette". This has been changed to "light-tight cassette" because the cassette's job is to keep light out so that only the screens' light exposes the film; it is not itself sensitive to light.

### Current recommendations for extra-oral film, intensifying screens and cassettes

- <mark>The fastest available film-screen combinations consistent with diagnostic results should be used.</mark>
- <mark>Ideally use rare-earth screens.</mark>
- <mark>The light colour sensitivity of the film should match the colour of light emitted by the screen.</mark>
- <mark>Regular QA procedures are required.</mark>

## Chemical processing

Processing converts the invisible latent image into a visible black/white/grey image.

### Development

<mark>Developing solution reduces the sensitised areas of the green emulsion to black metallic silver.</mark> <mark>The process is dependent on time and temperature.</mark> <mark>Over-developed films are too dark, while under-developed films are too pale.</mark>

### Fixation

<mark>Fixer solution removes all the unsensitised emulsion to reveal the clear/white base underneath.</mark> <mark>Inadequately fixed films may appear greenish-yellow or milky</mark> as emulsion has been left behind. <mark>Inadequately fixed films may also become brown over time.</mark>

### Current recommendations for processing

- <mark>Strict quality assurance procedures should be applied.</mark>
- <mark>Processing facilities should be properly cleaned, maintained and operated at the correct temperature.</mark>
- <mark>Developer should be changed at regular intervals as recommended by the manufacturer.</mark>

## X-ray equipment variables

| Variable | What it controls | Effect on the image |
|---|---|---|
| **kV** (kilovoltage) | <mark>The energy of the X-ray photons, and hence the penetrating power (quality) of the beam</mark> | <mark>Increasing kV will decrease the contrast</mark> between the light and dark parts of the radiographic image |
| **mA** (milliamperage) and **time** | <mark>The quantity of X-ray photons produced</mark> | <mark>Increasing the mA and time will increase the degree of blackening of the film</mark> |

# Module 21: How do you localise the position of an unerupted tooth?

*Practical techniques*

## What you should be able to do

- Describe the common radiographic methods used to localise unerupted teeth.
- Explain the principles of horizontal parallax and describe the practical techniques involved.
- Explain the principles of vertical parallax and describe the practical techniques involved.

## Key terms

- **Localisation:** <mark>the process of establishing radiographically the position or location of a buried object (e.g. an unerupted tooth)</mark>.
- **Parallax:** <mark>"the apparent displacement of an object because of two different positions of the observer", i.e. stationary objects seem to move as your point of view changes</mark>.

## Methods of localisation

### Two views taken at right angles or at different angles

An object can be pinpointed by taking two radiographs at right angles to each other.

- The **first radiograph** allows us to locate the object in the up/down and left/right dimensions.
- The **second radiograph** allows us to locate the object in the in/out dimension.

### Radiographic stents

A stent is a device, such as a plastic denture base plate, that the patient wears whilst being X-rayed. The stent contains metal markers at known points, so we can measure the location of teeth by looking at their relationship to these markers.

### Parallax

This method is used to assess the position of unerupted canines, odontomes and supernumeraries in the maxilla.

> **Lucy's note:** <mark>Clinical indications for using parallax</mark>:
> - assessment of the position of unerupted maxillary canines;
> - assessment of the position of unerupted maxillary odontomes and supernumeraries.

## The SLOB rule

Remember the acronym **SLOB**:

| Letters | Meaning |
|---|---|
| <mark>**S**ame</mark> – <mark>**L**ingual (palatal)</mark> | If the tooth moves in the same direction as the tubehead, it is lingual (palatal). |
| <mark>**O**pposite</mark> – <mark>**B**uccal</mark> | If the tooth moves in the opposite direction to the tubehead, it is buccal. |

- <mark>If the tooth seems to move in the same direction as the tubehead, then it is lingual to any superimposed object.</mark>
- <mark>If the tooth seems to move in the opposite direction to the tubehead, then it is buccal to any superimposed object.</mark>

## Horizontal parallax

<mark>You can either:</mark>

- take **two periapicals**: one centred on the upper central incisor, the other centred on the canine region (i.e. move the X-ray tubehead across horizontally); or
- take an **upper standard occlusal** centred on the midline, plus a **periapical** or an **upper oblique occlusal** centred on the canine region.

## Vertical parallax

Take a **dental panoramic radiograph** and an **upper standard occlusal** (i.e. one radiograph from the front, and one radiograph from the top).

## Localisation using Cone Beam Computed Tomography (CBCT)

CBCT gives us the ability to view slices of the patient and to move back and forth, up and down, and left and right through the slices. This makes localisation very easy.

CBCT machines with a small Field of View (FOV) are ideal for localising unerupted or supernumerary canines in the maxilla.

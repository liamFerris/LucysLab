# Module 24: What can you see?

*Radiological interpretation of lesions affecting the teeth*

## What you should be able to do

- Describe the optimum conditions for viewing radiographs, both digital and film.
- Identify the various radiographic appearances of dental caries.
- Identify the normal radiographic appearances of the periapical tissues.
- Identify the common radiographic appearances of periapical disease.
- Identify the normal radiographic appearances of the periodontal tissues.
- Identify the common radiographic appearances of periodontal disease.

## The objectives of interpretation

<mark>The objectives of interpretation are:</mark>

- to identify the presence or absence of disease;
- to provide information on the nature and extent of the disease;
- to enable the formation of a differential diagnosis.

## Viewing conditions

### Optimum digital viewing conditions

- A clean, bright, good-quality monitor
- The monitor checked using a test pattern
- A quiet, darkened room

### Optimum film viewing conditions

> **Correction:** The printed handout says "Optimum digital viewing conditions" for this second list. This has been changed to "Optimum film viewing conditions" because the items listed (a light viewing screen and blocking out light around the film) describe viewing film radiographs, not a digital monitor.

- An even, uniformly bright light viewing screen, preferably including an extra-bright spotlight
- A magnifying glass
- A quiet, darkened room
- An SDI X-ray viewer or black card to block the light surrounding the film

## Radiographic appearance of dental caries

(See Chapter 17 of your textbook for diagrams.)

Caries diagnosis requires a careful clinical examination before taking radiographs. Radiographs are one method for detecting carious lesions, but they do not provide information about the status of the carious process.

### Proximal caries (mesial/distal)

| Extent of lesion | Radiographic appearance |
|---|---|
| Confined to the enamel | A triangular radiolucency, with the point of the triangle towards the dentine |
| At the enamel-dentine junction (EDJ) | The triangular radiolucency in the enamel spreads along the EDJ |
| Involving the dentine | As well as the triangular radiolucency in the enamel, there is a semi-circular radiolucency projecting into the dentine |

### Other types of caries

| Type of caries | Radiographic appearance |
|---|---|
| Occlusal caries involving dentine | You usually cannot see evidence of caries in the enamel, but there is a semi-circular radiolucency in the dentine underneath the enamel |
| Surface caries (buccal/lingual) | Radiolucent oval-shaped lesions, because you are looking at the lesion end-on |
| Root/cervical caries | Usually radiolucent semicircles at the cervical margin |
| Secondary caries associated with a restoration or sealant (CARS) | Radiolucencies underneath restorations |

## Radiographic appearance of periapical disease

(See Chapter 18 of your textbook for diagrams.)

The periapical tissues normally appear as:

- a **black line**: the periodontal ligament space;
- a **white line**: the lamina dura of the tooth socket.

Periapical disease normally appears as a widening of the black line, as the periodontal ligament space fills with fluid. If the inflammation continues, the white line will disappear as the bone of the lamina dura is destroyed.

Chronic periapical disease may appear as a large radiolucency at the apex of the tooth. This radiolucency may be a periapical granuloma, a radicular dental cyst or, rarely, a discharging sinus.

## Radiographic appearance of periodontal disease

Normally, the distance from the cemento-enamel junction to the alveolar crest bone should be 2–3 mm.

Periodontal disease results in destruction of the bone, which shows radiographically as a radiolucent area where the alveolar bone should be.

- The percentage of interproximal bone loss can be used to assess the stage of the disease, from Stage I (mild) to Stage IV (very severe).
- Severe bone loss may show as a radiolucent area in the furcation of the roots.
- Localised vertical bone loss may show as a radiolucency in the bone extending down one root.

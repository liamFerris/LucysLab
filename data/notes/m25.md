# Module 25: What can you see?

*Radiological interpretation of lesions affecting the jaws*

## What you should be able to do

- Identify the main developmental defects of the teeth and jaws.
- Identify the various radiographic appearances of the main acquired diseases that can affect the jaws.
- Explain why benign and malignant disease present with different radiographic appearances.

## Developmental defects of the teeth

<mark>Most of these defects are obvious on radiographs.</mark>

### Number defects

- Too few teeth (hypodontia)
- Too many teeth (supernumeraries)

### Shape defects

- Extra roots
- Misshapen crowns
- Large pulp chamber (taurodont tooth)
- Compound odontomes (multiple, small teeth)
- Complex odontomes (a jumble of dental tissues, which appears as a radiopaque mass)
- Invaginated odontome (dens-in-dente)

### Position defects

- Unerupted maxillary canines
- Unerupted, impacted third molars
- Dilacerated (bent) incisors

## Developmental jaw defects

<mark>These defects are obvious on radiographs.</mark>

- Abnormal jaw size and shape:
  - too small;
  - too large.
- One side of the mandible may not develop, leading to a lop-sided appearance.

## Acquired diseases affecting the jaws

Bone can react to disease in two ways:

| Reaction | What happens | Radiographic appearance |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Resorption | Bone is removed | Radiolucent lesion |
| 2. Deposition | Bone is laid down | Radiopaque lesion |

### Why benign and malignant lesions look different

**Chronic or benign diseases** tend to be slow-growing, so the body has time to try to contain the lesion by laying down bone around it. This leads to a well-defined radiolucent area with a dense, radiopaque bony margin. These lesions are usually expansive, i.e. they shove teeth and bone out of the way as they grow.

**Acute or malignant diseases** tend to be fast-growing: too fast for the body to contain them by laying down bone. These lesions are usually poorly defined areas of radiolucency without a dense bony margin. They often destroy bone as they grow.

> **Correction:** The printed handout says "poorly defined areas of radiopacity". This has been changed to "poorly defined areas of radiolucency" because these lesions destroy (resorb) bone, and bone resorption appears radiolucent.

### Infection

| Type | Example | Radiographic appearance |
|---|---|---|
| Localised infection | <mark>Periapical granuloma</mark> | A well-defined radiolucency around the apex of a tooth |
| Spreading infection | <mark>Osteomyelitis</mark> | A poorly defined lucent area where the bone has been resorbed |

### Odontogenic cysts

- <mark>Radicular dental cyst</mark> (the most common type): round, circular and unilocular, with a well-defined bony outline at the apex of a non-vital tooth.
- <mark>Residual cyst</mark>: a radicular cyst left behind after the non-vital tooth has been extracted.
- <mark>Dentigerous cyst</mark>: a round, circular, unilocular area of radiolucency around the crown of an unerupted tooth.
- <mark>Odontogenic keratocyst</mark>: multilocular, with a 'soap bubble' appearance. Develops instead of a tooth.

### Non-odontogenic cysts

- <mark>Nasopalatine duct cyst</mark>: a developmental cyst which appears as a radiolucency in the midline of the anterior maxilla.

### Odontogenic tumours

- <mark>Ameloblastoma</mark>: multilocular, expansive and locally aggressive. Also has a 'soap bubble' appearance.

### Non-odontogenic tumours (benign)

- <mark>Osteoma</mark>: a well-defined lump of bone, easily seen on radiographs.
- <mark>Fibroma</mark>: arising from fibrous tissue.
- <mark>Neuroma</mark>: arising from neural tissue.

### Non-odontogenic tumours (malignant)

- <mark>Primary squamous cell carcinoma</mark>: the most common oral cancer, which can invade local bone and show up on radiographs as irregular destruction of bone.
- <mark>Osteosarcoma</mark>: a bone cancer which can be osteolytic (destroying bone) or osteogenic (depositing bone, with a characteristic 'sun-ray' appearance).
- <mark>Secondary metastatic deposit</mark>: a cancer which started in another part of the body and spread to the jaw bones. Typically appears as an irregular area of bone destruction.

### Giant cell lesions and bone cysts

- <mark>Central giant cell granuloma</mark>: a benign, multilocular, expansive lesion.
- <mark>Solitary bone cyst</mark>: a benign, irregular 'hole in the bone'.

### Fibro-osseous lesions

- <mark>Periapical cemento-osseous dysplasia</mark>: this lesion initially appears as radiolucencies around the apices of the teeth. Once the lesions calcify, they appear as opacities.
- <mark>Florid cemento-osseous dysplasia</mark>: usually affects the posterior quadrants, again changing from a radiolucency to a radiopacity.

# Module 4: What happens when X-rays hit you or the patient?

*The effects of X-rays on human tissue*

## What you should be able to do

- Explain the effects of X-rays on tissues, including the atomic interactions.
- Describe the process of ionisation.
- Explain how X-rays cause damage and describe the resulting effects.

## X-ray absorption in the tissues

### The photoelectric effect

<mark>This occurs when an X-ray photon collides with an inner-shell electron in the patient's tissue. All of the photon's energy is absorbed in the collision and the structure of the atom is destabilised. As the atom restabilises, it gives off energy in the form of heat and light.</mark>

The chance of an X-ray photon hitting an inner-shell electron depends on the atom's atomic number cubed (Z³). <mark>The higher the Z³, the more chance there is that the X-ray photon will be completely absorbed.</mark>

### The Compton effect

<mark>This occurs when an X-ray photon collides with an outer-shell electron in the patient's tissue. This time, the X-ray photon loses only some of its energy and is deflected. Again, the structure of the patient's atom is destabilised by the collision.</mark>

### Comparing the two effects

| | Photoelectric effect | Compton effect |
|---|---|---|
| Electron hit | Inner-shell electron | Outer-shell electron |
| Photon's energy | All absorbed | Only some lost |
| What happens to the photon | Completely absorbed | Deflected (scattered) |
| Effect on the atom | Destabilised | Destabilised |

### Ionisation

Both the photoelectric effect and the Compton effect result in an unstable atom (known as a positive ion) and a stray electron (known as a negative ion). <mark>This process is known as ionisation, hence X-rays are referred to as "ionising radiation".</mark>

## How X-rays cause tissue damage

### Direct damage

This occurs when an X-ray photon hits and ionises the genetic code in the patient's cells (the code is contained in DNA or RNA molecules). The damage may be severe enough to kill the cell outright, but is more likely to result in a subtle alteration to the code. This disruption to the genetic code may lead to a malignancy (in somatic cells) or a congenital abnormality (in reproductive cells).

### Indirect damage

This occurs when an X-ray photon hits and ionises other molecules in the patient's cells, most commonly water molecules. The resulting ions are highly reactive and go on to damage other parts of the cell. If an X-ray ionises a water molecule, then hydrogen peroxide and hydrogen gas are produced. Both are toxic substances.

## The damaging effects of X-rays

<mark>High doses of radiation can cause severe damage or even death.</mark>

<mark>The radiation doses used in dentistry are not high enough to cause deterministic effects, so we are mainly concerned about somatic stochastic effects.</mark>

<mark>Cancer is one example of a somatic stochastic effect. Even a tiny dose of radiation can cause a fatal cancer, so there is no such thing as a "safe dose".</mark>

# Module 7: How do you limit the dose to the patient and yourselves?

*Physical methods of dose limitation and practical radiation protection*

## What you should be able to do

- Explain the dose limitation principles of justification, optimisation and limitation.
- Describe the radiation dose limits for patients and dental staff.
- Describe the practical radiation protection measures used for patients.
- Describe the practical radiation protection measures used for yourself and other members of staff.

## Dose limitation principles

| Principle | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Justification** | With any medical radiation exposure, the benefits must outweigh the risks. |
| **Optimisation** | Steps must be taken to ensure that the radiation dose is As Low As Reasonably Practicable: the ALARP principle. |
| **Limitation** | The dose limits set by the ICRP must not be exceeded. |

## Radiation dose limits

### Patients

For patients who have a clear clinical need for an X-ray investigation, there are no set dose limits. However, the decision to carry out the investigation is the responsibility of the clinician, in your case a dentist. It is the dentist who must balance the risks and the benefits of exposing the patient to radiation.

### Staff

<mark>The law sets a dose limit for workers (20 mSv a year), but the guidance notes recommend a stricter dose constraint (0.3 mSv) for dental staff involved in radiography.</mark> <mark>Your exposure to X-ray radiation at work can be measured using a personal monitoring device.</mark>

> **Correction:** The printed handout says "The law sets a dose limit for non-classified workers (20mSv)". This has been changed to "The law sets a dose limit for workers (20 mSv a year)" because 20 mSv is the legal annual dose limit for employees in general; under IRR17, a worker likely to receive more than 6 mSv a year must be designated as a classified worker.

### Carers, comforters and the public

- <mark>The recommended dose limit for carers and comforters is 0.1 mSv per visit.</mark> <mark>Carers must also wear a lead apron.</mark>
- <mark>The limit for a member of the public is 1 mSv per year.</mark>

## Practical radiation protection

There are three main ways of reducing the radiation dose to patients and staff:

1. Clinical judgement
2. Equipment
3. Radiographic technique

### Clinical judgement

This is entirely the responsibility of your dentist. He or she must decide whether taking a radiograph is clinically necessary and whether the benefits of the radiation exposure outweigh the risk. <mark>Dentists should use "Selection Criteria for Dental Radiography" to help them.</mark>

### Equipment recommendations and regulations

**X-ray set**

- 70 kV
- Aluminium filtration
- Adjustable kV, mA and time, allowing short exposure times
- DC (constant potential)
- Long focus-to-skin distance (fsd) of 200 mm
- Rectangular collimation
- Critically examined and acceptance tested

**Image receptors**

- Intra-oral and extra-oral digital: solid-state or phosphor plates
- Intra-oral fast film: E/F speed
- Extra-oral film with rare-earth intensifying screens

### Radiographic technique

- Have adequate training and undertake CPD.
- Undertake radiography safely and accurately to avoid retakes, e.g. use image receptor holders with beam-aiming devices to produce geometrically accurate intra-oral images.
- Use patient positioning aids during panoramic radiography.
- Use the minimum number of projections.
- Optimise all exposure settings to ensure all doses are kept as low as diagnostically acceptable.
- Ensure all image processing (computer or chemical) is carried out to the highest standards so that images do not have to be retaken.
- Adhere to strict quality control procedures.
- Use "appropriate" protective lead aprons.

## Pregnant patients

If your patient is pregnant (or is likely to be pregnant), this must be taken into account by the practitioner when justifying a radiation exposure.

The point of physical radiation protection is to keep the dose to all patients as low as possible. No special measures need to be taken with pregnant patients unless the X-ray beam is aimed at their pelvis. <mark>This is highly unlikely in routine dental practice.</mark>

<mark>However, it may be worth offering the patient a protective lead apron, just for their own peace of mind.</mark>

## Physical methods of dose limitation to staff

For the safety of staff, the employer is required to:

- have contingency plans in the event of equipment malfunction and significant accidental or unintentional exposures;
- consult with a Radiation Protection Adviser (RPA);
- designate the "controlled area" or areas;
- ensure all staff are adequately informed, instructed and trained, i.e. they know the risks to their own health and follow the Golden Rules.

## Golden Rules

- <mark>Always</mark> stand outside the "controlled area".
- <mark>Never</mark> stand in the line of the main beam.
- If it is not practical to designate the entire practice as the "controlled area", <mark>the operator should always stand at least 1.5 m away from the X-ray equipment.</mark>
- <mark>Never</mark> hold the image receptor in the patient's mouth.
- <mark>Never</mark> hold the X-ray tubehead during the exposure.
- <mark>Always</mark> use X-ray equipment safely and in accordance with current guidance and good practice.

## Duties of employees

- <mark>You must never expose yourself or any other person in the practice to more radiation than is absolutely necessary.</mark>
- <mark>You must exercise reasonable care when working with X-ray radiation.</mark>
- <mark>If an incident or accident does happen, you must report it to your employer immediately.</mark>

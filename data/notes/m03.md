# Module 3: How are X-rays produced?

*X-ray production and X-ray generating equipment*

## What you should be able to do

- Explain how X-rays are produced, understand the atomic interactions involved, and explain why X-ray photons are not all the same.
- Describe the basic features of dental X-ray generating equipment.
- Understand the relevance of current recommendations and regulations concerning X-ray equipment.
- Explain the exposure variables (kV, mA and time) and how they affect the image.

## Components of dental X-ray equipment

| Component | Description |
|---|---|
| <mark>Tubehead</mark> | The main body of the set. |
| <mark>X-ray tube</mark> | A small glass vacuum tube where the X-rays are produced. On one side of the tube there is a tungsten wire filament, called the cathode. On the other side is a tungsten target (or anode) set into a block of copper. |
| <mark>Step-up transformer</mark> | An electrical device which converts ordinary mains voltage (240 V) to the high voltage required to produce X-rays (70,000 V, or 70 kV). |
| <mark>Oil</mark> | The whole tubehead is filled with oil to help remove the heat produced during an exposure. |
| <mark>Spacer cone</mark> | Used to aim the X-ray beam. |

## X-ray production

<mark>X-rays are created when high-speed electrons collide with the tungsten target.</mark>

When you push the button on the X-ray set, <mark>electrons are accelerated from the filament towards the tungsten target by a high voltage "push".</mark>

### Why X-ray photons are not all the same

The high-speed electrons can interact with the tungsten atoms in a range of ways, so they produce X-ray photons with a range of energies. They are produced in both a <mark>continuous spectrum</mark> and a <mark>characteristic spectrum of energies.</mark>

The overall energy of the X-ray photons depends on how fast the electrons fly into the tungsten target, and so <mark>is determined by the size of the high voltage "push", or "kV".</mark>

### Efficiency and heat

The process is very inefficient. <mark>Only one per cent of the electrons' energy is converted into X-rays. The other 99% is converted to heat, which is conducted away by the copper block and oil.</mark>

### Low-energy and high-energy photons

- <mark>Low-energy X-ray photons tend to be absorbed by the patient's soft tissues and can therefore cause damage.</mark>
- <mark>High-energy X-ray photons pass through the soft tissues to hit the film, so are more diagnostically useful.</mark>
- <mark>X-ray sets with a high kV rating (e.g. 70 kV) will produce more high-energy, diagnostically useful X-ray photons.</mark>
- <mark>The most harmful, lowest-energy X-ray photons can be removed by passing the beam through an aluminium filter.</mark>

## X-ray equipment variables

| Variable | What it controls | Effect on the image |
|---|---|---|
| kV (kilovoltage) | <mark>The energy of the X-ray photons, and hence the penetrating power (quality) of the beam.</mark> | <mark>Increasing kV will decrease the contrast between the light and dark parts of the radiographic image. Remember this as "kV kills contrast".</mark> |
| mA (milliamperage) and time | <mark>The quantity of X-ray photons produced.</mark> | <mark>Increasing the mA and time will increase the degree of blackening of the film.</mark> |

## Equipment recommendations and regulations

- <mark>Operating range of 60–70 kV.</mark>
- <mark>A 1.5 mm thick aluminium filter for X-ray sets below 70 kV, and 2.5 mm for sets above 70 kV.</mark>
- <mark>DC "constant potential"</mark> output (note: <mark>"potential" is another word for voltage</mark>).
- <mark>You may need to select 60 kV as opposed to 70 kV to ensure adequate contrast.</mark>
- <mark>Rectangular collimation.</mark>
- <mark>Maximum diameter of 60 mm for circular collimators.</mark>
- <mark>200 mm focus-to-skin distance (fsd), or a long spacer cone.</mark> A 300 mm fsd is being used in the most modern equipment.
- Audible and visual warning signals. <mark>Timer switches must be continually depressed throughout the exposure.</mark>
